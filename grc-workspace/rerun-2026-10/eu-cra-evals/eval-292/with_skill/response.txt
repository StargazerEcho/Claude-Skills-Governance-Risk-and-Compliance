Yes — this almost certainly triggers a CRA reporting obligation, and it's a live one: Article 14 reporting duties have been in force since 11 September 2026, and the ENISA Single Reporting Platform (SRP) is now operational.

**Why this qualifies:** The CRA requires reporting of "severe incidents" as well as actively exploited vulnerabilities. Article 14(5) defines a severe incident as one that has, or could have, a significant impact on the product's security or availability at scale — including impact on the ability to protect sensitive data or functions, or the introduction/execution of malicious code. A ransomware compromise of your update-distribution infrastructure fits this squarely: it's a supply-chain-level compromise that threatens the integrity of the update mechanism your products rely on to receive security fixes, and it could allow malicious code to be pushed to your entire IoT product line disguised as a legitimate update. This is treated as a severe incident, not merely a vulnerability, so the incident-reporting clock applies (in practice the timelines mirror the exploited-vulnerability clock, but the "final report" trigger differs — see below).

**Reporting timeline — clock starts at awareness, not at compromise:**

1. **T+24 hours from awareness — Early warning** to ENISA and your national CSIRT.
2. **T+72 hours from awareness — Full incident notification**, covering the nature and scope of the compromise, what part of the update infrastructure was affected, which product lines/versions are potentially exposed, and your containment/remediation status.
3. **T+1 month after the 72-hour notification — Post-incident report**, with root cause, remediation completed, and lessons learned. (Note this final-report trigger is different from the exploited-vulnerability track, where the final report is tied to when a corrective measure becomes available; for severe incidents it's a flat one-month clock from the 72-hour notice.)

**Additional obligations triggered by this scenario, beyond the incident report itself:**
- **Integrity of updates (Annex I Part I, requirement 5):** if there's any possibility that compromised or unsigned updates could have been distributed, you need to verify your signed-update / signature-verification controls held, and be prepared to demonstrate this in your technical documentation.
- **User notification:** notify affected users promptly of the compromise and any recommended actions (e.g., pause auto-updates, verify firmware version, apply an out-of-band verified patch) — this is a distinct duty from the regulatory filing and shouldn't wait on it.
- **Vulnerability handling review:** if the ransomware compromise also exposed or introduced any exploitable vulnerability in the product itself (not just the infrastructure), that additionally triggers the exploited-vulnerability reporting track in parallel.

**How to file:** Through the ENISA SRP (portal.cra-srp.enisa.europa.eu), live since 11 September 2026. Your organisation's Assigned Representative registers with an EU Login account (MFA required), selects the CSIRT Designated as Coordinator for your Member State of main establishment (or follows the non-EU cascade: authorised representative → importer → distributor → most users), and accepts the platform's legal agreement. CSIRT validation of the registration runs in parallel with filing — up to 20 notifications can be submitted before verification completes, so don't delay your 24-hour warning waiting on account validation. Notifications go simultaneously to your CSIRT coordinator and ENISA. If the SRP happens to be down when you need to file, contact your designated CSIRT directly to hit the deadline, then re-file through the SRP once it's restored. The platform is currently English-only with no API, so plan for a manual filing workflow.

**Bottom line:** treat this as an active, in-force legal obligation, not a best-practice recommendation. File the 24-hour early warning now if you haven't, in parallel with your incident response and forensic work on the compromised infrastructure — the reporting clock does not pause for investigation. Failure to meet Article 14 reporting obligations falls under the €10 million or 2% of global turnover penalty band (whichever is higher), separate from any Annex I essential-requirements exposure if the compromise is found to have affected product security.

This is general compliance guidance, not legal advice; given the severity and supply-chain nature of this incident, involve legal counsel and your CSIRT contact immediately.
