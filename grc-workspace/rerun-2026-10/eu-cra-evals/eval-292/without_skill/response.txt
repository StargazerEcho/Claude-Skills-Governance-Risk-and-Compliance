Yes — this is very likely reportable, and probably on the same accelerated clock as an actively exploited vulnerability. Here's how to think about it under the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847), whose incident-reporting obligations (Article 14) have applied since 11 September 2026.

Is it in scope? Article 14(4)-(5) requires manufacturers of products with digital elements to notify a "severe incident having an impact on the security of the product" — not just vulnerabilities in the product's code. A compromise of your update-distribution infrastructure is a textbook severe incident: it directly threatens the integrity of the update mechanism that is supposed to be a core security control for your IoT product line, and ransomware on that infrastructure raises the realistic possibility that update packages could be tampered with, delayed, or used to distribute malicious payloads to deployed devices. Even if you have not yet confirmed that any malicious firmware was actually pushed to customer devices, the compromise of infrastructure that is integral to the products' security (their patching pipeline) is the kind of event the CRA's severe-incident concept is meant to capture, because it affects the products' security posture and the trust customers place in your update channel.

Reporting clock — same three-stage structure as vulnerability reporting:
- Early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware of the incident, indicating whether it's suspected to be caused by unlawful/malicious conduct (ransomware obviously qualifies) and, if known, cross-border reach.
- Incident notification within 72 hours, updating the early warning with an assessment of severity and impact, and any indicators of compromise if available.
- Final report within one month of the incident notification, providing a detailed description, the root cause, mitigation/remediation applied, and cross-border impact where relevant.

Where to file: through ENISA's single reporting platform (Article 16), which is designed to relay the notification to the CSIRT designated as your coordinator and to relevant market surveillance authorities. If the sector-specific platform integration is not yet smooth, route through your national CSIRT/single point of contact — the clock still runs from "awareness," not from platform availability.

Don't overlook overlap with other reporting regimes. Ransomware on infrastructure could also trigger obligations under:
- NIS2, if you or your update-distribution provider qualifies as an essential or important entity (digital infrastructure, managed service provider, or manufacturer of certain critical products can be in scope) — NIS2 has its own 24h/72h/1-month incident notification cascade to national CSIRTs, so where both regimes apply you may be able to align your reporting rather than duplicating separate, uncoordinated notices, but check with your CSIRT on how they want the notices linked.
- GDPR, if the ransomware exposed personal data (customer accounts, device telemetry, credentials) — that runs on a separate 72-hour clock to your Data Protection Authority.
- Contractual/customer notification duties, and depending on your jurisdiction, sector-specific breach laws.

User notification: independent of the regulatory filings, Article 14(8)-style obligations mean you should inform affected users/customers about the incident and any protective action they should take — for example, pausing automatic updates, verifying signatures on any pending update, or watching for anomalous device behavior, especially if there's any chance a tampered update could have gone out before you locked down the infrastructure.

Immediate operational priorities, since the reporting clock is unforgiving: (1) nail down your "became aware" timestamp; (2) isolate/rebuild the update-distribution pipeline and verify no malicious packages were signed or distributed; (3) fire the 24-hour early warning now if you haven't; (4) start assembling severity/impact data for the 72-hour follow-up; (5) prepare customer-facing guidance. Given the ransomware angle and possible personal-data exposure, get legal counsel and your CSIRT involved in parallel — the interplay between CRA, NIS2, and GDPR notification duties is one area where the exact sequencing and content requirements are still being worked out in practice, and national CSIRT guidance may add specifics beyond what the regulation text itself spells out.
