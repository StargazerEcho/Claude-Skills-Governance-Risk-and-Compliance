This is exactly the scenario the EU Cyber Resilience Act's (CRA, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) early-warning reporting obligations were built for, and the relevant part of that regime (Article 14) has been in force since 11 September 2026 — so as of mid-September 2026 you are squarely inside its scope, even though the CRA's main body of obligations (CE marking, essential requirements, full conformity assessment) doesn't become generally applicable until 11 December 2027.

What you must do, and on what clock:

1. Actively exploited vulnerability notification (Article 14(1)-(3)). As the manufacturer of a product with digital elements placed on the EU market, once you become aware that a vulnerability in your firmware is being actively exploited, you must notify ENISA (which shares the information with the relevant national CSIRT and, where relevant, market surveillance authorities) on a strict three-stage clock:
   - Early warning: within 24 hours of becoming aware, giving basic information — that the vulnerability exists and is under active exploitation, and (if known) the member states affected.
   - Vulnerability notification: within 72 hours, with more detail — general information about the vulnerability, its severity, and, where available, any mitigating or corrective measures users can take.
   - Final report: no later than 14 days after a corrective/mitigating measure becomes available, describing the vulnerability, its severity and impact, and (where applicable) how it was resolved, including any cross-border information if the exploitation crossed borders.

2. Notification channel. Reporting is meant to go through a single reporting platform that ENISA is required to operate under Article 16, feeding the vulnerability information to the national CSIRT designated as coordinator under the CRA and, in parallel, to market surveillance authorities. In practice, many manufacturers will route through their national CSIRT/single point of contact if the ENISA platform channel isn't yet fully operational for their sector — but the legal clock runs regardless of platform status, so don't wait on platform issues to start your internal countdown.

3. User and public notification (Article 14(8)). In parallel with your regulator-facing reporting, you have an independent obligation to inform affected users of the vulnerability, the risks, and any corrective or mitigating measures they should take, "without undue delay" after becoming aware and, where appropriate, warn users in a manner that lets them protect themselves (patch, workaround, disabling a feature, etc.). Where necessary for security, the CRA allows you to delay or limit public/user disclosure to the extent strictly necessary, but you can't use that as a blanket excuse for silence.

4. Remediate. In parallel with reporting, you are expected to be actively working the fix — the CRA's vulnerability-handling requirements (coordinated disclosure, providing security updates free of charge for the support period, maintaining an SBOM, etc.) describe the substantive obligations that go with the reporting clock, and market surveillance authorities can and will ask about your remediation timeline when you file.

5. Documentation and internal governance. Preserve a clear internal timeline of when you "became aware" (this is the trigger for the 24-hour clock and will be scrutinized), who made the assessment, and what was reported when. Regulators expect manufacturers to have a documented vulnerability-handling and incident-response process already in place — if you don't, build one now, because the obligation to have such a process is not itself deferred to 2027; only the certification/CE-marking machinery is.

Practical next steps for you, today: (a) confirm your "aware since" timestamp and calculate your 24-hour deadline; (b) identify your national CSIRT/ENISA contact point and file the early warning immediately if you haven't; (c) start drafting the 72-hour follow-up with severity/mitigation detail; (d) prepare a user notice; (e) keep remediation moving toward a fix so your 14-day final report has real content. Given the legal and reputational stakes of an actively exploited IoT vulnerability, I'd also loop in outside counsel and your national CSIRT's guidance pages, since procedural specifics of the ENISA platform are still being operationalized and national implementation details can vary.
