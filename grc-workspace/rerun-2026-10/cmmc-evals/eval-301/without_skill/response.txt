Context heading into 2026: CMMC 2.0 had been finalized in the 32 CFR rule (effective late 2024) and DoD was working through the 48 CFR contract-clause rulemaking needed to actually start requiring certifications in contracts. Throughout 2025 there was substantial industry commentary - from the Defense Industrial Base Association, National Defense Industrial Association, and various trade groups - arguing that the assessment ecosystem (C3PAOs, assessor capacity, cost burden on small and mid-size contractors) wasn't ready to scale, and that the rule's requirements around Level 2/3 assessments, POA&M timelines, and flow-down obligations to subcontractors needed clarification or softening. There was also a recurring theme in Congress and among some administration officials about right-sizing compliance burden for small businesses without weakening actual cybersecurity outcomes. Any "reform task force" report in this space would plausibly be addressing some combination of: assessor/C3PAO capacity constraints, cost and timeline relief for small businesses, clarity on scoping and CUI definitions, treatment of legacy systems, and possibly changes to the POA&M/conditional certification structure.

What you should do about it, regardless of what's actually in that report:

1. Don't treat any "reform" or "pause" signal as a green light to stop working. Contract clauses referencing NIST SP 800-171 (via DFARS 252.204-7012) and the SPRS self-assessment/scoring requirements have been live obligations for years, independent of CMMC certification timelines. Reform of the CMMC certification program does not retroactively suspend your existing contractual cybersecurity obligations.

2. Get the primary source before you act. Don't rely on secondhand summaries or vendor marketing about what a task force report says. Pull the actual report or the associated DoD/DFARS rulemaking notice from official channels (acquisition.gov, DoD CIO's CMMC site, Federal Register) and read the actual proposed changes, effective dates, and comment periods.

3. Map any proposed changes against your current compliance posture. If the report proposes changes to assessment levels, timelines, or scope, run a gap analysis: what does it change for contracts you currently hold vs. contracts you're bidding on, and does it affect your internal SSP, POA&M, or assessment scheduling.

4. Keep your NIST SP 800-171 control implementation and System Security Plan moving forward as your baseline. Whatever administrative path the certification program takes, the underlying technical and process requirements to protect CUI aren't going away - if anything, industry pressure has generally pushed toward more automation and continuous monitoring expectations, not less.

5. Engage your contracting officers and industry associations for authoritative interpretation. DFARS clause language and DoD guidance documents (not task force commentary) are what actually bind you contractually.

6. If you're advising leadership, frame this as: "here's what we know is confirmed and binding today, here's what's proposed and still moving, and here's our no-regret posture that holds up either way."
