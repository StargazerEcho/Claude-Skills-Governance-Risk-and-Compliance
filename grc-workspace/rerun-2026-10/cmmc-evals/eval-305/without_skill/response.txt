An "enclave" strategy (sometimes called a CUI enclave, security enclave, or scoped environment) is one of the most common and legitimate ways to reduce CMMC/NIST 800-171 scope, and it can work well - but it requires real architectural discipline, not just a network diagram label. Here's how scoping actually works and what you need to get right.

The core scoping principle:
CMMC/NIST SP 800-171 scope is defined by where Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) is processed, stored, or transmitted, plus any assets that could affect the security of that CUI even if they don't directly touch it. DoD's CMMC scoping guidance (published alongside the CMMC assessment guides) breaks assets into categories:
- CUI Assets: systems that process, store, or transmit CUI directly. These are fully in scope and must meet all applicable 800-171 requirements.
- Security Protection Assets (SPAs): systems that provide security functions for the CUI environment (e.g., your SIEM, your identity provider/MFA system, your patch management server) even if they don't hold CUI themselves. These are in scope, though the assessment focuses on the security-relevant functions they provide.
- Contractor Risk Managed Assets (CRMAs): assets that can potentially reach or affect the CUI environment (e.g., they're on the same network segment but aren't supposed to process CUI) but are managed through your risk-based security policies. These are documented in your SSP but assessed differently than full CUI assets.
- Out-of-Scope Assets: assets that are physically or logically separated from the CUI environment such that they cannot access, store, or affect it. To claim something is out of scope, you have to be able to demonstrate that separation, not just assert it.

Why an enclave helps:
If you build a properly segmented CUI enclave, only the enclave (and the security tools protecting it) fall under CMMC Level 2/3 assessment scope. The rest of your corporate network - email, HR systems, general engineering environments that never touch CUI - can be treated as out of scope, which dramatically reduces the assessment burden, cost, and ongoing compliance overhead. This is a very common and DoD-endorsed strategy specifically because building 800-171 controls (encryption, access control, audit logging, incident response, etc.) across an entire enterprise network is often far more expensive and disruptive than building them into a purpose-built, smaller environment.

What actually makes an enclave defensible (this is where people get it wrong):
1. Genuine logical and/or physical separation. This typically means a distinct network segment with its own firewalled boundary, its own identity/access controls (ideally separate directory or at minimum enforced conditional access), and controlled data flow points. A VLAN with a shared Active Directory and shared file shares to the rest of the network usually does NOT hold up as a true boundary.
2. Controlled ingress/egress. Every path CUI could take into or out of the enclave needs to be identified and controlled - file transfer mechanisms, email gateways, removable media, remote access/VPN, printing, and even things like copy-paste over remote desktop sessions if RDP crosses the boundary. If your engineers can freely move files or email CUI out of the enclave, the boundary isn't real.
3. No unmanaged crossover of privileged/security tools. If your SIEM, identity provider, or endpoint management tool covers both the enclave and the general network, those tools become Security Protection Assets and pull a meaningful slice of your broader environment into scope. Many organizations either deploy dedicated instances of these tools for the enclave or very carefully scope what those tools can see/touch.
4. Endpoint control. Devices that access the enclave (including via remote/virtual desktop) need to meet the same requirements as if they were CUI assets, unless you're using a strategy like a Virtual Desktop Infrastructure (VDI) or Desktop-as-a-Service where the actual CUI processing happens entirely within the enclave and the endpoint only receives a rendered screen with no local storage, clipboard, or print capability - this is one of the more popular ways smaller contractors shrink true scope, since the physical laptops accessing the enclave can then be treated with lighter requirements (though they're still generally at least a CRMA, not fully out of scope, and you need to document why).
5. People and process boundary. Only personnel who need CUI access should have enclave credentials, and your policies need to actually prevent and detect CUI moving outside the enclave (e.g., DLP tooling, restricted file-sharing permissions, training).
6. Documentation. Your System Security Plan (SSP) needs a clear, accurate network diagram and narrative showing the CUI boundary, what's inside (CUI Assets and SPAs), what's adjacent (CRMAs), and why everything else is genuinely out of scope. Assessors will test your claimed boundary, not just read your diagram - expect them to probe whether CUI can actually leak across it.

Common failure modes to avoid:
- "Enclave" that's really just a folder permission on a shared file server with no network segmentation.
- Shared helpdesk/IT admin accounts with access to both the enclave and general network, creating a privileged crossover path.
- Email: if CUI can be emailed from inside the enclave to any general corporate mailbox, or vice versa, your enclave doesn't actually contain CUI flow.
- Backup systems: your enclave's backups need to stay within the same protected boundary (or an equivalently protected one) - a common gap is backing up the enclave to a general enterprise backup target.
- Assuming reduced scope also means reduced control rigor - the 110 requirements still apply in full to everything inside the enclave; you're reducing the footprint, not the requirement depth.

Practical next steps:
- Do a data flow mapping exercise first: where does CUI actually enter your organization (email, portals, physical media, contract deliverables) and where does it need to go for your team to do the work. Design the enclave around that minimum necessary footprint.
- Decide on an architecture pattern: dedicated segmented network with its own endpoints, or VDI/DaaS approach where thin clients/general laptops access a virtual environment that fully contains the CUI - VDI is often the fastest way for smaller contractors to shrink scope without buying entirely separate hardware for every enclave user.
- Get your identity, remote access, and monitoring tooling scoped deliberately - decide up front whether they're dedicated to the enclave or shared, and understand the scope consequence of that choice.
- Have your scoping decisions and boundary reviewed by someone experienced in CMMC assessments (ideally before you invest in build-out) - a boundary that looks reasonable to your IT team can still fail assessor scrutiny if data flow paths weren't fully traced.
- Document everything in the SSP with a clear boundary diagram, and be ready to demonstrate (not just assert) the separation during assessment.

Bottom line: enclaving is a sound, widely used strategy to shrink your compliance footprint, but the benefit only materializes if the segmentation is real, actively enforced, and fully documented - a boundary that exists on paper but leaks in practice will pull your entire network back into scope during assessment.
