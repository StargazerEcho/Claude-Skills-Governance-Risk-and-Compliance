No - and this is one of the most important misconceptions to clear up quickly, because it can lead to real contractual and legal exposure if leadership acts on it.

First, a caveat: I don't have verified information about a specific "CMMC Phase 2 suspension" - my knowledge has a cutoff around January 2026, and I can't confirm current-as-of-today details about DoD rulemaking status without being able to browse the web in this session. So I'll answer the underlying compliance question in general terms that hold true regardless of the specific status of CMMC's phased rollout, and flag what you should verify independently.

Here's the key distinction that matters regardless of what's happening with CMMC certification timelines: CMMC and NIST SP 800-171 are related but not the same obligation, and they don't turn on and off together.

NIST SP 800-171 compliance has been a contractual requirement since DFARS clause 252.204-7012 took effect (originally 2017, with the current version in force for years now). If you hold or bid on DoD contracts that involve Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), you are already required to:
- Implement the 110 (now 97 in Rev. 3, depending on which revision your contract cites) security requirements in NIST SP 800-171
- Maintain a System Security Plan (SSP) and Plans of Action and Milestones (POA&Ms) for any unimplemented controls
- Submit a Basic Assessment score to SPRS (Supplier Performance Risk System)
- Flow down equivalent requirements to subcontractors handling CUI

None of that depends on CMMC certification deadlines. CMMC was built as a verification mechanism layered on top of 800-171 - essentially a way for DoD to have third parties (or self-attestation, depending on level) confirm that what you've been claiming in SPRS is actually true. If CMMC's certification rollout ("Phase 2" referring to the timeline for requiring Level 2 assessments in contracts) is paused, delayed, or under reform, that affects when and how you get externally verified - it does not suspend the underlying 800-171 requirement or your existing SPRS self-assessment obligation, which are independently enforceable through your contracts and through the False Claims Act if you misrepresent your compliance status.

So the practical risks of deprioritizing 800-171 work based on a CMMC pause:
1. You may still be contractually required to maintain an accurate SPRS score today, and letting your actual security posture drift out of alignment with what's on file is a compliance and legal risk (potential False Claims Act exposure for misrepresenting cybersecurity compliance - DOJ has an active Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative pursuing exactly this).
2. New contracts and modifications may still reference 800-171/CMMC requirements even during a "pause" in broader rulemaking.
3. Reform/pause periods are historically when the requirements get clarified and tightened, not relaxed. Treating a pause as a reason to stop work often means scrambling later when a compressed effective date arrives.
4. Your competitive position: primes and contracting officers increasingly favor subs who can demonstrate mature compliance regardless of enforcement mechanics; falling behind hurts past performance and teaming opportunities.
5. Insurance, customer contracts (including commercial customers who now reference CMMC/NIST 800-171 as a security benchmark), and cyber insurance underwriting increasingly look at this posture independent of DoD enforcement timing.

What I'd recommend instead of deprioritizing:
- Verify the actual, current DFARS clause language and any interim rule status directly from the Federal Register or DoD's CMMC program page - don't rely on secondhand claims about what's "suspended."
- Continue closing your 14 (or however many) POA&M items on a risk-prioritized basis - highest-risk controls first (access control, incident response, audit logging, media protection typically carry the most risk).
- Keep your SSP and SPRS score current and accurate regardless of certification deadlines - accuracy of the self-assessment score is itself an enforceable requirement.
- Use any pause in the certification schedule productively: it's a gift of time to actually finish remediation work properly rather than scrambling for a deadline, not a signal to stop.

Bottom line: treat CMMC certification timeline news as informational about when you'll be externally verified, not as a reason to change your underlying NIST 800-171 obligations, which remain in force via contract and carry real legal risk if neglected.
