Here's how the SPRS (Supplier Performance Risk System) scoring methodology works under NIST SP 800-171, and what your 14 unimplemented practices mean in practice.

The scoring model:
NIST SP 800-171 (Rev. 2, which is what most current DFARS 252.204-7012/7020 scoring is built around) has 110 security requirements across 14 control families. DoD's scoring methodology (published by OUSD(A&S)/DoD CIO, sometimes called the "NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Methodology") assigns a starting score of 110 points, representing full implementation of every requirement. For each requirement you have NOT implemented, you subtract points - but not evenly. Each of the 110 requirements has a pre-assigned point value (1, 3, or 5 points) based on its assessed criticality/impact on the overall security posture. A few high-value controls (like FIPS-validated cryptography, multifactor authentication, or restricting/monitoring remote access) are weighted at 5 points; mid-tier controls are typically 3 points; lower-impact or more procedural controls are typically 1 point. So your score is calculated as: 110 minus the sum of point values for every requirement not fully implemented, which can result in scores ranging from 110 down to -203 (there's a floor around there because some controls can trigger multiple deductions when unimplemented in ways that also negate credit for other partially-dependent controls).

What 14 unimplemented practices means for your score:
Fourteen missing practices is not automatically a small or large deduction - it entirely depends on which 14. If those 14 happen to be mostly 1-point items (e.g., certain low-impact policy/documentation requirements), you might be looking at a score in the 90s. If several of those 14 are 5-point items (e.g., multifactor authentication for privileged/remote access, encryption of CUI at rest or in transit, boundary protection, incident response capability), you could be looking at a much lower score - possibly requiring point deductions well into double digits from just a handful of gaps. You need to map your specific 14 gaps against the DoD Assessment Methodology scoring template (it's a publicly available spreadsheet/matrix listing every one of the 110 requirements with its assigned point value) to get your actual number - I can't give you a precise score without knowing which controls are on your gap list.

What you're required to do with this:
1. Calculate your actual score using the current scoring template and submit (or update) it in SPRS. This is a Basic Assessment, self-generated, and it must reflect your true current implementation status - not an aspirational one. Submitting or maintaining an inaccurate SPRS score is exactly the kind of thing DOJ's Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative has pursued under the False Claims Act.

2. Build a Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M) for each of the 14 unimplemented practices. A POA&M documents the specific gap, the planned remediation steps, responsible party, and target completion date. Under current policy, POA&Ms have to represent realistic, time-bound plans - a POA&M isn't a permanent substitute for implementation, and depending on the contract clause version there may be a required timeframe (historically discussed as roughly 180 days for closing critical POA&M items, though you should confirm the specific timeline requirement in your current contract).

3. Understand SPRS score validity/currency. Assessments (and thus your submitted score) are generally treated as current for a defined period (commonly referenced as three years) unless your security posture materially changes - but you should re-submit whenever you close POA&M items to reflect improvement, since contracting officers can and do check your score before award decisions on new work.

4. Prioritize remediation of the highest-point-value gaps first. Since the score is a straightforward point subtraction, closing your five highest-value gaps will move your score much more than closing five 1-point gaps. This also generally aligns with actual risk reduction, since the weighting reflects DoD's judgment about security impact - MFA, encryption, access control, and incident response capabilities tend to be the biggest score movers and the biggest real-world risk reducers.

5. Watch for a Medium or High Assessment. DoD (via DCMA's DIBCAC) can conduct government-led assessments that supersede or validate your self-assessment; if there's a discrepancy between your self-reported score and what an actual assessment finds, that's a significant compliance and contractual problem, so make sure your self-assessment is honest and well-documented, not optimistic.
