Here's how a GRC consultant typically walks a client through POA&M remediation timelines and continuous monitoring (ConMon) expectations under FedRAMP. This is based on the well-established Rev5/traditional FedRAMP framework; if your organization is operating under a FedRAMP 20x pilot, some mechanics (e.g., automated KSI validation replacing parts of manual ConMon) may differ, so confirm specifics against your governing ConMon plan and PMO guidance.

POA&M basics: The Plan of Action and Milestones is the living document that tracks every known weakness or deficiency in your system — findings from the initial assessment, vulnerability scans, penetration tests, incident-driven findings, and control deviations — along with remediation plans, responsible parties, and target completion dates. FedRAMP requires you to maintain and submit updated POA&Ms on a recurring basis (typically monthly) to your authorizing agency or the PMO, and reviewers actively track whether you're closing items within required timeframes.

Standard remediation timelines (these are the widely cited FedRAMP targets, derived from vulnerability severity):
- Critical vulnerabilities: remediate within 30 days of detection.
- High vulnerabilities: remediate within 30 days of detection (in earlier guidance sometimes bucketed with critical; current FedRAMP guidance has trended toward treating Critical and High similarly, often both at 30 days, though some references use a tighter window for Critical — verify the exact current SLA in your ConMon plan and the FedRAMP Continuous Monitoring Strategy Guide).
- Moderate vulnerabilities: remediate within 90 days of detection.
- Low vulnerabilities: remediate within 180 days of detection, or per your organization's risk-based justification.

These clocks start from the date of detection (typically the scan date), not the date the finding is written up or reported, which is a common compliance pitfall — organizations that delay internal triage effectively shorten their own remediation window without realizing it.

Deviation requests: If you cannot remediate within the standard window — due to vendor patch unavailability, business-critical system dependencies, compensating controls, or operational constraints — you must submit a formal risk adjustment/deviation request to your AO, with justification, compensating controls, and a revised target date. These aren't automatic; the AO can reject the deviation and require faster remediation, and a pattern of repeated deviation requests without real progress is a common trigger for authorization concerns or agency escalation.

POA&M submission cadence: Monthly POA&M updates are the norm, submitted alongside your monthly ConMon deliverables. Each submission should show: new findings added, findings closed with evidence, status changes on open items, and updated target dates for anything slipping. Agencies and 3PAOs specifically scrutinize aging open items — findings sitting open well past their target date without documented deviation are a red flag in annual assessments and can jeopardize your ATO.

Continuous monitoring deliverables more broadly, beyond POA&M, typically include:
- Monthly vulnerability scan results (infrastructure, database, web application) and a summary of scan-to-POA&M reconciliation.
- Monthly or quarterly significant change requests for any architecture, control, or personnel changes that affect your security posture.
- Annual security assessment — essentially a mini re-assessment by your 3PAO covering a representative control sample, updated SSP, updated SAR, incident response test results, and contingency plan test results.
- Annual FedRAMP Annual Assessment package submission to your authorizing agency, which is the formal checkpoint where continued authorization is affirmed or challenged.
- Incident reporting — actual security incidents must be reported to US-CERT/CISA and your agency within specified timeframes (historically as fast as 1 hour for confirmed incidents under federal incident reporting requirements, though exact thresholds depend on incident category), separate from the routine POA&M cycle.

Practical recommendations:
1. Build internal SLAs tighter than the FedRAMP minimums (e.g., target 21 days for Critical/High) so that normal internal friction doesn't blow your compliance deadline.
2. Automate scan-to-POA&M ingestion where possible; manual reconciliation is the most common source of missed findings and stale entries.
3. Track POA&M aging as a standing metric in your internal risk dashboard, not just at submission time, so leadership sees overdue items before the agency does.
4. Treat deviation requests as an exception process requiring real justification and compensating controls, not a routine way to manage a backlog — auditors and AOs notice patterns of habitual deviation requests.
5. Rehearse your annual assessment readiness continuously rather than scrambling annually — your ConMon discipline throughout the year is what actually determines how smooth (or painful) the annual assessment turns out to be.

Bottom line: FedRAMP's POA&M and ConMon regime is built around risk-tiered remediation SLAs (roughly 30/90/180 days for Critical-High/Moderate/Low), monthly reporting discipline, and an annual re-assessment checkpoint — the organizations that struggle are almost always the ones that let internal triage delay the remediation clock or that treat deviation requests as routine rather than exceptional.
