The "November 2026 problem" your board is sensing relates to the oversight mechanics DORA built around Critical ICT Third-Party Providers (CTPPs), and specifically to how the Lead Overseer framework interacts with a US-established provider.

Background: DORA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554) became applicable on 17 January 2025. One of its most consequential features is the direct oversight regime for CTPPs — ICT providers (cloud, core banking platforms, data analytics, etc.) that the European Supervisory Authorities (EBA, ESMA, EIOPA), acting through the Joint Committee, designate as "critical" to the financial sector based on systemic-impact criteria (substitutability, number of financial entities relying on them, degree of criticality of the services supported, etc.). Once designated, a CTPP is assigned a "Lead Overseer" — one of the three ESAs — which then has powers to request information, conduct inspections, issue recommendations, and ultimately, in cases of persistent non-compliance, recommend that financial entities suspend or terminate their contractual relationship with that provider.

If your core-banking SaaS provider was designated in November 2025, the practical oversight machinery generally phases in over roughly 12 months from designation: the Lead Overseer conducts its initial risk assessment, issues its oversight plan, and — critically — if it identifies material weaknesses in the provider's risk management, it can issue formal recommendations. Providers are typically expected to respond and remediate within a defined window, often around 60 days, and the Lead Overseer publishes/communicates outcomes to the competent authorities of the financial entities using that provider.

So the "November 2026" milestone is most likely the point at which the first full 12-month oversight cycle for your provider concludes — meaning the Lead Overseer's initial assessment, any recommendations, and the enforcement escalation path all come to a head. This matters to your board for several reasons:

1. Recommendation exposure: If the Lead Overseer has flagged gaps (e.g., in subcontracting transparency, exit strategy robustness, concentration risk, or security controls) and the provider hasn't fully remediated, your national competent authority may require you, as a "user" financial entity, to demonstrate you've factored that risk into your own ICT risk management framework and your Register of Information. You could face supervisory pressure to diversify or accelerate exit planning even if you have no direct contractual leverage over the CTPP.

2. US jurisdiction friction: Because the provider is US-established rather than EU-established, DORA's oversight regime relies on cooperation arrangements between EU authorities and third-country regulators, plus contractual commitments the provider must accept (subjecting itself to EU oversight, granting access/audit rights, etc.). If the provider has been slow or incomplete in accepting these obligations, tensions between US and EU legal/regulatory expectations (data access, subpoena conflicts, US CLOUD Act issues) can surface as unresolved risk by month 12 — precisely when your own supervisors will be looking for evidence of contingency planning.

3. Contractual and exit-plan deadlines: DORA requires financial entities to have documented exit strategies and to test them. If oversight findings suggest the provider is a single point of failure or has concentration risk across the EU financial sector, your regulator may specifically ask you to show a credible, tested exit plan (alternative provider, data portability, reversibility of services) — not just a paper policy — by around this anniversary point.

What your board should actually do before November 2026:
- Obtain, if possible, any communications the provider has received from its Lead Overseer (providers are often required to inform client financial entities of material oversight findings).
- Refresh your criticality/concentration risk assessment for this provider in your Register of Information.
- Stress-test your exit strategy: confirm data extraction formats, transition timelines, and a genuinely viable alternative.
- Update your board risk register and ICT risk management framework documentation to show active monitoring of this specific designation, not just generic third-party risk language.
- Engage your national competent authority proactively if you anticipate gaps, rather than waiting to be asked.

In short: November 2026 is the point where oversight scrutiny of a newly designated CTPP typically intensifies and where any unresolved US/EU jurisdictional or remediation gaps become visible to your own supervisor — treat it as a checkpoint, not a deadline that resolves itself.
