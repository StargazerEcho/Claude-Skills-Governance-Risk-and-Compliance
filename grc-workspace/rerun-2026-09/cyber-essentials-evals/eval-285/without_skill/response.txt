Two separate questions here - let's take them one at a time.

The "free insurance"
Yes, this is real, but it's more limited than the marketing suggests. When you certify to Cyber Essentials (basic) through most IASME-licensed certification bodies, IASME includes a complimentary cyber liability insurance element as part of the certification package - historically this has been up to £25,000 of cover (the exact figure and terms have varied over time and by provider, so check the current wording from whichever certification body you use). Key caveats:
- It's typically only available to organisations with turnover under a certain threshold (historically around £20 million) - larger companies usually don't qualify for the free cover, though they can often purchase enhanced cover as an add-on.
- It's usually only offered to UK-registered/domiciled organisations.
- It's a modest, entry-level policy intended to soften small-business risk, not a substitute for a proper standalone cyber insurance policy - it typically covers things like incident response costs, business interruption, and liability up to the stated limit, with various exclusions.
- Cyber Essentials Plus doesn't add a different or larger insurance benefit by itself - the free insurance is generally tied to the underlying basic CE certificate.
- It is not guaranteed for every organisation or every certification body arrangement - terms, provider (historically this has been arranged via IASME's insurance partner), and eligibility can change, so get written confirmation of what applies to you at the point of certifying rather than relying on general scheme reputation.

Bottom line: treat it as a genuine but modest bonus, not your primary cyber risk transfer mechanism - if you handle sensitive government or MoD data, you'll likely need proper commercial cyber insurance regardless, sized to your actual risk exposure.

What an MoD prime will expect from you
Being a subcontractor/supplier to an MoD prime brings a materially higher bar than a typical government department contract, for a few structural reasons:

1. Cyber Essentials (and often Plus) as a floor, not the ceiling. MoD contracts frequently reference the Defence Cyber Protection Partnership (DCPP) framework and a risk-based approach called "Cyber Security Model" (which superseded/absorbed elements of the older DEFSTAN 05-138 and the "Defence Cyber Protection Partnership" model). Depending on the risk level of the contract and the type of data you'll handle, you may be required to go beyond basic CE - Cyber Essentials Plus is common, and for higher-risk contracts (those involving Official-Sensitive information, defence-related IP, or higher-impact data), the prime may require alignment with additional standards.
2. Data classification awareness. MoD/defence contracts operate under the Government Security Classifications Policy (OFFICIAL, OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, SECRET). Even "OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE" data carries handling requirements well beyond generic good practice - expect requirements around data marking, need-to-know access controls, secure disposal, and potentially restrictions on where data can be stored/processed (e.g., no offshore cloud regions, no personal devices for certain data).
3. Supply chain due diligence. Primes typically flow down contractual security requirements (often via DEFCON 658 or similar defence conditions in the contract, and increasingly referencing the Cyber Security Model) requiring subcontractors to demonstrate their own compliance and sometimes to cascade the same requirements further down their own supply chain.
4. Incident reporting obligations. Expect contractual clauses requiring you to report security incidents to the prime (and potentially MoD) within tight timeframes, and to cooperate with any investigation.
5. Personnel security. Depending on the contract, staff with access to certain information may need baseline personnel security standard (BPSS) checks at minimum, and possibly security clearance (SC) for more sensitive work.
6. Physical and system security controls beyond CE's technical scope - things like clear desk policies, controlled printing, visitor management, and secure destruction of media, especially if you're handling any physical documents or removable media related to the contract.
7. Right to audit. Primes and MoD frequently reserve contractual rights to audit your security controls, sometimes going beyond what a CE Plus assessment covers.

Practical next step: ask your prime contact directly (or check the tender's security schedule/annex) which specific standard applies - "Cyber Essentials," "Cyber Essentials Plus," or a defence-specific requirement referencing the Cyber Security Model risk profile - because the answer genuinely varies by contract risk level, and getting this wrong (assuming basic CE is sufficient when Plus or additional defence-specific controls are required) is a common and costly mistake for first-time MoD suppliers.
