This is a serious finding — walk it through the full ladder methodically rather than jumping to a conclusion, but don't be surprised if it lands at material weakness.

**Step 1 — Get the facts pinned down precisely.** You need, at minimum: exact termination date (February — get the day), exact date superuser access was finally revoked, what "superuser" actually means in this ERP (SAP_ALL-equivalent, DBA rights, ability to post journal entries, modify master data, or all of the above), and — critically — an activity log review covering the entire exposure window (February through Q3 discovery) to determine whether the account was actually used, and if so, for what. Also pull how the termination was missed: was there no deprovisioning ticket at all, did HR fail to notify IT, or did IT receive the request and not act? That root-cause detail drives both the classification nuance and the remediation plan.

**Step 2 — Likelihood and magnitude.** Likelihood: a live superuser account with no owner and no monitoring for roughly six-plus months (February through Q3, spanning at least one quarter-end and possibly your fiscal year-end depending on your calendar) is a "reasonable possibility" fact pattern almost by definition — there was nothing structurally preventing misuse. Magnitude: superuser/admin rights in an ERP typically mean unrestricted ability to post, modify, or delete transactions and master data across the GL — that's gross, unbounded exposure to every significant account the ERP touches, not a bounded dollar figure. Under AS 2201, you evaluate magnitude as maximum potential misstatement, gross exposure, not the (probably zero) actual loss you hope the activity log shows.

**Step 3 — Compensating controls.** Ask precisely: was there a detective control operating at sufficient precision to catch inappropriate use of this account during the window — e.g., a privileged-access activity review, a journal entry review that would catch unusual manual postings, a reconciliation that would surface unauthorized master-data changes? If no compensating control existed at the right precision, there is nothing to reduce the severity. A general "we review the balance sheet monthly" review is very unlikely to be precise enough to catch, say, a fraudulent vendor master-data change or an unauthorized manual journal entry — test this control's actual precision, don't just note its existence.

**Step 4 — Aggregation.** Check whether this is an isolated miss or a symptom: how many other terminated-user exceptions turned up in this UAR cycle or prior cycles? A single miss might evaluate differently than a pattern of termination-deprovisioning failures, which would aggregate across the population and push severity up. Also check whether this UAR is the first one that would have caught it — if terminations aren't tested continuously (the skill's guidance is to test terminations and privileged access continuously, not just at the quarterly UAR checkpoint), that's itself a design gap in your monitoring cadence, separate from this one instance.

**Step 5 — Classification.** Based on the facts as stated — a terminated privileged user retaining live superuser ERP access for roughly six months, spanning at least one quarter-end, with no indication yet of a precise compensating control — this pattern matches the canonical material weakness example: reasonable possibility of a material misstatement not being prevented or detected timely, magnitude unbounded absent a clean activity-log result and a demonstrated precise compensating control. If your activity log review comes back completely clean AND you can demonstrate a compensating control (e.g., an independent, precise transaction-level review) operated throughout the window, you have a real argument to step this down to significant deficiency — but the burden is on demonstrating that, not assuming it. Don't classify until the activity review is done; that's the single fact most likely to move this up or down the ladder.

**Reminder on ITGC evaluation logic**: this is an access-control ITGC failure with no direct misstatement itself — you evaluate it through what it undermines, i.e., every account and transaction the ERP superuser role could touch during the exposure window. That's why the activity/transaction review, not just the access-control fact pattern, is the deciding evidence.

**Next steps**: complete the activity log review now, document compensating-control testing (not just existence), draft the classification memo with AS 2201 Appendix A / Reg S-X Rule 1-02(a)(4) citations, and if this lands at significant deficiency or material weakness, route it to the audit committee (written communication either way) and, if material weakness, prepare it for 10-K Item 9A disclosure with a remediation plan and status. Root-cause the deprovisioning process break itself (HR-to-IT handoff, ticketing, or termination-checklist gap) — fixing just this one account without fixing the process invites a repeat finding, and repeat filers make up the majority of adverse ICFR assessments.
