This vendor combines two of the higher-risk offboarding profiles at once — a live OAuth integration into Salesforce plus retained customer contact data (PII) — so treat this termination with Tier 1/2 rigor even if the vendor itself is "just" a marketing tool, and don't let the offboarding process end at "cancel the subscription." Structure it around the standard checklist: access, data, technical debris, contractual, organisational, verification.

**1. Access revocation — start here, and start with OAuth.** This is the single most important step given the integration profile. Go into Salesforce's Connected Apps / OAuth grants and explicitly revoke the vendor's authorized access — don't rely on the vendor-side "disconnect" button alone, since a token can persist if only deauthorized on one side. Also: revoke any API keys issued to the vendor, remove any dedicated integration user/service account created for the connection, pull the vendor out of SSO/IdP group assignments, and check for any IP allow-list entries or webhook endpoints registered for this integration. This step matters because of a live precedent: the Salesloft Drift breach (Aug 2025) showed that stolen OAuth tokens from one marketing/sales-adjacent SaaS tool exposed Salesforce data at 700+ downstream organizations — a lingering, un-revoked or improperly-scoped OAuth grant is exactly the fourth-party blast-radius risk this skill flags as a Tier 1 integration-risk item, and it's the most common thing offboarding checklists miss.

**2. Data.** Inventory what customer contact data the vendor holds (contact records synced from Salesforce, campaign engagement history, any enriched/appended data they may have added). Request: (a) return of the data in an agreed format if you need it for records, and (b) certified deletion — get this in writing, ideally with a certificate of destruction, and confirm the timeline for their backup-cycle expiry (deletion from production systems is not the same as deletion from backups, which may persist for weeks). Confirm whether the vendor used any sub-processors for enrichment or storage (email verification services, data append vendors are common in martech) and that deletion flows down to them too — check the sub-processor list from the original DPA if one exists.

**3. Technical debris.** Beyond the OAuth grant itself: DNS records (CNAMEs for tracking domains, SPF/DKIM entries if the vendor sent email on your behalf), any webhooks configured to push Salesforce events to the vendor, embedded tracking scripts/pixels on your website or in email templates, and any scheduled sync jobs or middleware (e.g., Zapier-style connectors) that still reference the vendor's endpoints.

**4. Contractual.** Confirm what the contract obligates on exit: any termination-assistance period, final invoice reconciliation, and records-retention requirements that might require you (not the vendor) to keep certain data for a defined period. Check survival clauses — confidentiality and audit rights typically survive termination and may still let you request evidence of deletion after the relationship ends.

**5. Organisational.** Notify internal stakeholders (marketing, sales ops, whoever owned the Salesforce admin relationship) that the integration is dead so no one is surprised by broken syncs; update your vendor register/inventory to reflect termination; feed any lessons from this vendor relationship (e.g., if the OAuth scope was broader than necessary, or if data retention took longer than expected to confirm) back into your tiering and due-diligence criteria for future martech vendors — OAuth scope should be a standing due-diligence question for any new SaaS integration going forward, not just at termination.

**6. Verification — the step most programs skip.** Thirty days after offboarding, go back into Salesforce's login/API history and connected-apps audit log and confirm there is zero access from the vendor's integration — no API calls, no token refresh attempts. This closing check is the difference between "we think we revoked access" and confirmed proof, and it's the cheapest, highest-value step in the whole checklist because it catches the exact failure mode (a token that looked revoked but wasn't) that caused real-world downstream breaches in the incidents referenced above.

Given the customer-PII exposure, also confirm whether this termination triggers any breach-notification-adjacent obligations under your own privacy program (e.g., if data wasn't properly returned/deleted on schedule) and log the final deletion confirmation as evidence in your vendor file.
