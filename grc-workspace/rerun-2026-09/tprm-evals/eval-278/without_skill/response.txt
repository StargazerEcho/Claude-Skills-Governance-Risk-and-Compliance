There are two distinct compliance regimes at play here: GDPR governs the DPA and sub-processor review, while the health data for your US benefits program brings in HIPAA-adjacent considerations even though GDPR itself doesn't define "health data" the same way. Let me walk through both.

1. GDPR DPA requirements (Article 28)
Any SaaS vendor processing personal data on your behalf as a processor must be bound by a Data Processing Agreement that includes, at minimum:
- Subject matter, duration, nature and purpose of processing, type of personal data, and categories of data subjects - this scoping should match what the vendor actually does, not generic boilerplate.
- Processing only on documented instructions from you (the controller), including for international transfers - the vendor cannot process for its own purposes.
- Confidentiality commitments for personnel with access to the data.
- Security measures appropriate to the risk (Article 32) - encryption, pseudonymization, ongoing confidentiality/integrity/availability/resilience, and a process for regularly testing security measures.
- Conditions for engaging sub-processors: either specific prior authorization or general authorization with notice and the right to object to new sub-processors. The DPA must require the vendor to flow down the same data protection obligations to sub-processors via contract.
- Assistance obligations: helping you respond to data subject access requests (DSARs) and fulfill other Chapter III rights (access, rectification, erasure, portability, etc.).
- Assistance with Article 32-36 obligations: security, breach notification, DPIAs, and prior consultation with supervisory authorities where required.
- Return or deletion of personal data at the end of the contract, at your choice, and deletion of existing copies unless law requires storage.
- Audit rights: the vendor must make available information necessary to demonstrate compliance and allow for audits/inspections by you or an auditor you mandate.
- International transfer mechanism if data leaves the EEA (SCCs, adequacy decision, or binding corporate rules), plus a transfer impact assessment if using SCCs post-Schrems II.
- Breach notification timeline - typically the vendor must notify you "without undue delay" so you can meet your own 72-hour obligation to the supervisory authority.

2. Sub-processor list review
When reviewing the vendor's sub-processor list:
- Confirm there is a current, accessible list (many vendors publish this on a trust/security page or as a DPA annex) and a mechanism for change notification (e.g., 30 days' notice with a right to object).
- Check the location of each sub-processor and whether adequate transfer mechanisms are documented for those outside the EEA/UK.
- Verify each sub-processor's role matches its listed function (e.g., a cloud hosting provider vs. a customer support tool vs. an analytics provider) and assess whether the categories of data flowing to each are proportionate to that role.
- Confirm the vendor has DPAs in place with each sub-processor with equivalent obligations flowed down.
- Flag any sub-processors that seem unnecessary or overly broad in access, and ask the vendor to justify or restrict scope.
- Check for a right to object to new sub-processors and understand what happens if you object (termination right, alternative processor, etc.).

3. Employee health data for the US benefits program
This introduces additional considerations beyond GDPR:
- If any EU/UK employees are involved, health data is a "special category" of data under GDPR Article 9, requiring a specific legal basis beyond Article 6 (e.g., explicit consent, or processing necessary for employment/social security law obligations) plus heightened security measures. Confirm the vendor's DPA and security posture explicitly address special category data, not just generic personal data.
- For US employees, GDPR generally won't apply unless the vendor or your organization is otherwise in scope (e.g., you're an EU entity, or you're monitoring EU data subjects), but health data tied to a US benefits program is often subject to HIPAA if the plan is a group health plan and the vendor acts as a Business Associate. In that case, you likely need a separate Business Associate Agreement (BAA) covering PHI handling, breach notification under HIPAA's Breach Notification Rule, and minimum necessary use.
- State privacy laws (e.g., CCPA/CPRA and other US state laws) may also classify health data as sensitive personal information, triggering additional contractual and consent requirements.
- Practically: determine whether the benefits vendor is acting as a HIPAA Business Associate (likely, if administering a self-insured or fully-insured group health plan) and get a BAA executed in addition to, not instead of, the standard DPA/security addendum, since the two serve different legal frameworks.

Bottom line: treat this as a dual-track review - GDPR DPA adequacy and sub-processor governance for any EU-touching data, and a HIPAA BAA plus health-data-specific security controls for the US benefits data. Don't assume one document (the DPA) covers both; they typically need to be separate agreements referencing consistent security and breach-notification standards.
