Two separate compliance regimes are in play here — GDPR Art. 28 for the general processor relationship, and HIPAA for the employee health-data piece — and a DPA does not satisfy the second one, so treat this as two checklists, not one.

**1. GDPR Art. 28(3) DPA checklist.** Go clause-by-clause and mark each present/absent/deficient:
- **Documented instructions** — processing only on the controller's documented instructions, including for international transfers; check the DPA names the specific processing purposes and doesn't leave the vendor free to reuse data for its own purposes (e.g., model training) without separate written consent.
- **Confidentiality** — personnel authorized to process data are bound by confidentiality obligations.
- **Security (Art. 32 reference)** — the DPA should reference or incorporate the technical/organizational measures (encryption, access control, pseudonymization where relevant); vague "commercially reasonable" language is a deficiency — request specifics or an incorporated security exhibit.
- **Sub-processor authorization (Art. 28(2))** — check whether the vendor uses specific authorization (each named sub-processor approved individually) or general authorization (blanket approval with an objection right and advance notice period, ideally 30 days). General authorization without a meaningful objection right/notice window is a common deficiency.
- **Flow-down (Art. 28(4))** — sub-processors must be bound by data-protection obligations equivalent to the DPA itself; ask the vendor to confirm this contractually, not just operationally.
- **Data subject rights assistance** — the vendor must assist with access/erasure/portability requests reasonably promptly.
- **Breach notification** — timeline and channel; the vendor's SLA needs to leave you enough runway to meet your own 72-hour GDPR notification clock to the supervisory authority.
- **Deletion/return at termination** — confirm both the primary deletion and the backup-expiry timeline are specified.
- **Audit rights** — right to audit or rely on independent reports (SOC 2/ISO) with a right of inquiry into exceptions.

**2. Sub-processor list review.** Beyond the authorization mechanism above, check the actual list for: fourth-party concentration (are multiple critical functions routed through the same hyperscaler — a DORA-style concentration concern even outside financial services), transfer destinations and mechanisms (are any sub-processors outside the EEA, and if so what transfer mechanism — SCCs, adequacy decision — is documented), and the change-notification lead time (30 days is reasonable practice; anything shorter than 14 days doesn't give you meaningful time to object or reassess).

**3. The US employee health-data piece needs a BAA, not a DPA.** This is the answer-completeness flag worth stating explicitly: a GDPR DPA is a distinct legal instrument from a HIPAA Business Associate Agreement, and having one does not substitute for the other. If this vendor will create, receive, maintain, or transmit ePHI in connection with your US benefits program, HIPAA requires satisfactory assurances via a BAA before that data flows — 45 CFR 164.308(b)(1). The BAA must address (per 164.314(a)): the vendor's compliance with the Security Rule for the ePHI it handles; a requirement that any of its own subcontractors touching that ePHI sign equivalent BAAs (the chain flows down exactly like GDPR Art. 28(4) sub-processor flow-down, just under a different statute); and security-incident/breach reporting obligations (breach notification under 164.410 on top of whatever the DPA's GDPR-oriented breach clause says).

**Practical sequencing:** don't let health data start flowing under the umbrella of the existing GDPR DPA. Confirm with the vendor (a) whether they already offer a standard BAA (most benefits-adjacent SaaS vendors do), (b) get it executed before any ePHI is transmitted, and (c) check whether the same sub-processors that appear in the GDPR sub-processor list also touch the health-data workflows — if so, each of those needs to be covered by the BAA's subcontractor flow-down too, not assumed to be covered because they're already in the GDPR list. Tier this vendor accordingly: dual data categories (EU personal data + US ePHI) generally push a vendor to Tier 1 due-diligence depth regardless of size, given the combined regulatory exposure.
